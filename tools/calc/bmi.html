What BMI is, and what it isn't

Body Mass Index (BMI) is a simple ratio of weight to height, used since the 1970s as a quick screening tool for weight categories. It was originally developed by the Belgian statistician Adolphe Quetelet in the 1830s as a way to describe the "average man." The World Health Organization adopted it in the 1970s as a standard reference for population-level health statistics.

BMI is useful because it's cheap, easy, and correlates with body fat at the population level. It is not a diagnostic tool. It cannot tell the difference between fat and muscle, does not show where fat is stored, and does not account for age, sex, ethnicity or fitness. A muscular athlete and an inactive person can have the same BMI with completely different health profiles.

Treat BMI as one signal among many. Waist circumference, blood pressure, blood sugar, cholesterol, physical activity and family history all matter more for individual health assessment than BMI alone.

How BMI is calculated

Metric formula

BMI = weight (kg) ÷ height² (m)

Example: 70 kg at 1.75 m
BMI = 70 ÷ (1.75 × 1.75) = 70 ÷ 3.0625 = 22.9

Imperial formula

BMI = 703 × weight (lb) ÷ height² (in)

Example: 154 lb at 69 inches
BMI = 703 × 154 ÷ 4761 = 22.7

The two formulas produce the same result. The constant 703 accounts for the unit conversion between pounds-inches and kilograms-metres.

What is BMI Prime?

BMI Prime is your BMI divided by 25, the upper limit of the healthy weight range. It's a way of expressing how far above or below the healthy ceiling you are.

  • BMI Prime below 0.74 — underweight
  • 0.74 to 0.99 — healthy range
  • 1.0 to 1.19 — overweight
  • 1.20 to 1.39 — Obese Class I
  • 1.40 and above — Obese Class II or III

BMI Prime is useful because it directly answers "how much above the healthy ceiling am I?" A BMI Prime of 1.10 means you're 10% over the healthy upper limit.

What is the Ponderal Index?

The Ponderal Index is weight (kg) ÷ height³ (m). Unlike BMI, which uses height squared, the Ponderal Index uses height cubed. This makes it more accurate for very tall or very short individuals, because BMI tends to over-classify tall people as overweight and under-classify short people.

Normal Ponderal Index range for adults is roughly 11 to 14 kg/m³. Below 11 suggests underweight; above 14 suggests overweight.

For most people, BMI and Ponderal Index give similar answers. They diverge most for individuals at extreme heights.

When BMI is misleading

BMI was designed for population studies, not individual health assessment. It fails in several common situations:

  • Athletes and bodybuilders. Muscle is denser than fat. A rugby player at 100 kg and 1.85 m has a BMI of 29.2 — technically overweight — with body fat often below 15%.
  • Older adults. Muscle mass declines with age while fat mass increases. A 70-year-old and a 30-year-old with the same BMI can have very different body compositions.
  • Children and teenagers. Growth and puberty change body composition rapidly. Children need BMI-for-age percentiles, not adult thresholds.
  • Pregnant women. BMI is not meaningful during pregnancy. Healthcare providers use weight-gain guidelines specific to pre-pregnancy BMI.
  • People of Asian descent. Health risks appear at lower BMI levels. Use the Asian standard.
  • People with very high or very low muscle mass. Both extremes distort BMI. Waist circumference and body fat percentage are more informative.

Better measures to use alongside BMI

No single measurement tells the full story. These are more informative in combination with BMI:

Waist circumference

Measures abdominal fat, which is more strongly linked to cardiovascular and metabolic risk than total body fat. Measure at the narrowest point, usually just above the navel.

Waist-to-height ratio

Keep your waist circumference below half your height. A 170 cm person should aim for a waist below 85 cm. This is one of the simplest and most accurate screening tools.

Waist-to-hip ratio

Waist circumference divided by hip circumference. Above 0.90 for men or 0.85 for women suggests increased risk.

Body fat percentage

Measured via DEXA scan, hydrostatic weighing or bioelectrical impedance. Healthy ranges are roughly 10–20% for men and 18–28% for women, varying with age.

BMI and health risk

Population studies consistently show that BMI outside the healthy range correlates with increased risk of several conditions:

  • Underweight (BMI below 18.5) — nutritional deficiencies, weakened immune function, osteoporosis, fertility issues.
  • Overweight (BMI 25–29.9) — elevated risk of type 2 diabetes, hypertension, cardiovascular disease and some cancers.
  • Obese (BMI 30+) — substantially increased risk across cardiovascular, metabolic and musculoskeletal conditions.

Correlation is not causation at the individual level. Many people with a BMI above 25 are metabolically healthy. Many with a BMI in the "healthy" range have risk factors that BMI doesn't capture. Blood pressure, blood sugar, cholesterol, waist circumference and lifestyle matter more than BMI for predicting individual outcomes.

How to use BMI as a screening tool

If you want to use BMI meaningfully:

  1. Track it over time. A single reading is less informative than a trend. Sustained upward or downward movement is worth paying attention to.
  2. Combine it with waist measurement. BMI plus waist circumference is far more informative than either alone.
  3. Look at the whole picture. Blood pressure, fasting glucose, cholesterol and physical activity are stronger predictors of health than BMI.
  4. Don't panic over a single number. BMI is a population tool, not a personal verdict. Talk to a healthcare provider about what your measurements mean for your specific situation.
  5. Speak to a professional before making changes. If BMI prompts you to consider a diet or exercise plan, involve a doctor or registered dietitian. Sudden weight changes in either direction deserve medical attention.

What this calculator can't do

Being honest about limits is more useful than pretending they don't exist. This calculator does not:

  • Diagnose any health condition. BMI is a screening tool, not a diagnostic one. It cannot tell you whether you have diabetes, heart disease or any other condition.
  • Measure body fat. It uses weight and height only. For body fat percentage, use DEXA, hydrostatic weighing or a calibrated bioimpedance scale.
  • Replace medical advice. Decisions about diet, exercise, medication or treatment should involve a qualified healthcare professional.
  • Work for children under 20. Use a pediatric BMI-for-age percentile calculator instead.
  • Be accurate during pregnancy. Prenatal weight gain guidelines replace BMI during pregnancy.
  • Account for muscle mass. Athletic individuals should use body fat percentage and waist measurements instead.
  • Predict individual outcomes. It correlates with population-level risk but does not predict what will happen to a specific person.

Common questions

What is BMI?

Body Mass Index (BMI) is a measure of body fat based on height and weight. It is calculated as weight in kilograms divided by height in metres squared. BMI has been used since the 1970s as a quick screening tool for weight categories.

What is a healthy BMI?

For most adults, a BMI between 18.5 and 24.9 is considered healthy. Below 18.5 is underweight, 25 to 29.9 is overweight, and 30 or above is obese. Under the Asian standard, 18.5 to 22.9 is normal and 23 to 24.9 is overweight.

Does BMI work for everyone?

No. BMI does not distinguish muscle from fat, so athletes may show as overweight. It is not accurate for children, pregnant women, or the elderly. Different thresholds apply for people of Asian descent.

How do I calculate BMI manually?

Divide your weight in kilograms by your height in metres squared. For example, 70 kg and 1.75 m: 70 ÷ (1.75 × 1.75) = 22.9, which is in the healthy range.

What is BMI Prime?

BMI Prime is your BMI divided by 25, the upper limit of the healthy range. A BMI Prime below 1.0 means you are within the healthy range. Above 1.0 means you are over the upper limit.

What is the Ponderal Index?

The Ponderal Index is weight in kilograms divided by height in metres cubed. Unlike BMI, it does not over-classify tall people as overweight. Normal range is roughly 11 to 14 kg/m³.

What is the difference between the WHO and Asian standards?

The WHO standard uses 25 as the overweight threshold. The Asian standard uses 23, because people of Asian descent tend to develop health problems at lower BMI levels. Many health authorities in India, China and Japan use the Asian thresholds.

Why is my BMI high even though I exercise?

BMI cannot distinguish muscle from fat. A muscular athlete can have a BMI of 30 with very low body fat. For athletic individuals, waist circumference and body fat percentage are more informative measures.

Is BMI accurate for children?

No. Children and teenagers need BMI-for-age percentiles, which account for growth and development. The adult BMI thresholds used here apply only to people aged 20 and above.

Can I use this BMI calculator during pregnancy?

No. BMI is not accurate during pregnancy. Your healthcare provider will use weight gain guidelines specific to your pre-pregnancy BMI instead.

What is a healthy waist circumference?

For men, a waist circumference below 94 cm (37 inches) is considered healthy. For women, below 80 cm (31.5 inches). Above 102 cm for men or 88 cm for women indicates substantially increased health risk. Waist-to-height ratio (keeping waist under half your height) is a simpler guide.

Is BMI used to diagnose health conditions?

No. BMI is a screening tool, not a diagnostic one. It identifies weight categories that may correlate with health risks, but it cannot diagnose a condition. A doctor will consider BMI alongside other factors including waist circumference, blood pressure, blood sugar, cholesterol and family history.

What should I do if my BMI is outside the healthy range?

Speak to a healthcare professional. They can assess your overall health, help you set realistic goals, and recommend a plan that suits your circumstances. This calculator provides information, not medical advice.

Does BMI apply to all ethnic groups equally?

No. People of Asian descent tend to develop type 2 diabetes and cardiovascular disease at lower BMI levels than people of European descent. Lower thresholds apply. Some health bodies also recommend adjusted thresholds for other ethnic groups.
