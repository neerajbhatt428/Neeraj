Calculate your daily calorie needs in a minute

This is a free calorie calculator that finds your BMR (Basal Metabolic Rate — the calories your body burns at complete rest) and TDEE (Total Daily Energy Expenditure — the calories you actually burn in a day, including exercise and daily movement). Once those are known, the calculator gives you a daily target for losing weight, maintaining it, or gaining muscle, along with protein, carb and fat targets for five different diet styles.

It's used by people starting a weight-loss plan, athletes building muscle, and anyone who wants to understand why their weight is drifting up or down. Everything runs in your browser — no account, no upload, no tracking of your health data.

How to use the calculator

  1. Enter age and sex. These affect BMR because muscle mass and hormonal profiles differ.
  2. Pick an activity level. This is the single most important input — it multiplies BMR by 1.2 to 1.9. Choose the level that matches your average week, not your best week.
  3. Enter weight and height. Use metric or imperial — the other fills automatically. Current weight is more accurate than your goal weight.
  4. Choose a goal. Maintain, lose, or gain. The calculator adjusts the daily target to match your chosen weekly rate of change.
  5. Pick a diet split. Balanced, high-protein, low-carb, keto or vegetarian. Each gives a different protein / carb / fat ratio.
  6. Read your targets. See BMR, TDEE, daily target, macro grams, macro split, weekly and monthly totals, and a meal-by-meal breakdown.
  7. Copy or share. Copy the summary or macros as text, or share a link that pre-fills the calculator with your inputs.

What is BMR?

Basal Metabolic Rate is the number of calories your body burns at complete rest — just to stay alive. Breathing, circulating blood, repairing cells, regulating temperature, digesting — all of it. Even if you stayed in bed all day without moving, you would burn this many calories.

BMR accounts for roughly 60–70% of the calories most people burn in a day. The remaining 30–40% comes from physical activity and the thermic effect of food (the energy used to digest what you eat).

BMR is driven mostly by body size and composition — larger bodies burn more at rest, and muscle burns more than fat. Age and sex also matter: BMR declines slowly with age, and men tend to have more muscle mass than women of the same size.

What is TDEE?

Total Daily Energy Expenditure is BMR multiplied by an activity factor that accounts for everything else you do in a day. It is the total calories you burn, and it is the number you would eat to maintain your current weight.

TDEE is a moving target. It changes with:

  • Activity level. The obvious one — more exercise means more calories burned.
  • Body weight. As you lose weight, TDEE drops. A smaller body burns fewer calories at rest and moves less mass during exercise.
  • Muscle mass. More muscle means higher BMR and higher TDEE.
  • Age. TDEE declines by roughly 1–2% per decade after 30.

This is why a calorie target that produced weight loss in month one might produce a plateau by month three. Recalculate every 4–8 weeks as your weight changes.

The Mifflin-St Jeor equation

This calculator uses the Mifflin-St Jeor formula, published in 1990. It is widely considered the most accurate BMR equation for the general population, and it is the formula recommended by the Academy of Nutrition and Dietetics.

For men

BMR = 10 × weight (kg) + 6.25 × height (cm) − 5 × age + 5

For women

BMR = 10 × weight (kg) + 6.25 × height (cm) − 5 × age − 161

Then

TDEE = BMR × activity factor

Older formulas like Harris-Benedict (1919) tend to overestimate by 5–15%. Newer formulas like Katch-McArdle require body fat percentage, which most people don't know accurately. Mifflin-St Jeor is the best compromise for most users.

Most people overestimate their activity level. If you sit at a desk for 8 hours but lift weights 4 times a week, you are probably "lightly active" or at the low end of "moderately active", not "very active".

When in doubt, pick the lower multiplier. If you're losing weight faster than expected, increase the activity level. If you're gaining, decrease it.

Calorie deficit for weight loss

One kilogram of body fat equals roughly 7,700 calories. So a daily deficit translates to weight loss at a predictable rate:

Daily deficitWeekly lossMonthly loss
250 cal/day~0.25 kg~1 kg
500 cal/day~0.5 kg~2 kg
750 cal/day~0.75 kg~3 kg
1,000 cal/day~1 kg~4 kg

Most guidelines recommend 0.5 to 1 kg per week. Faster loss tends to rebound and strips muscle along with fat. Never go below your BMR for extended periods — that can slow your metabolism, cause gallstones, and lead to nutrient deficiencies.

Safe minimums: Most health bodies warn against sustained intakes below 1,200 calories for women and 1,500 for men without medical supervision.

Calorie surplus for muscle gain

Muscle gain is slower than fat loss. A surplus of 250 to 500 calories per day supports steady gains without excessive fat accumulation. Combined with resistance training and adequate protein, you can typically gain 0.25 to 0.5 kg of muscle per month.

Larger surpluses do not produce faster muscle gain — they mostly add fat. The body has a limited rate at which it can synthesise new muscle tissue, and for most people that limit is well below the surplus that would cause rapid weight gain.

Beginners and younger lifters can gain muscle faster than experienced lifters. If you've been training for years, expect gains at the lower end of the range.

Protein, carbs and fat

Macronutrients are the three main components of food. Each has a different calorie density and function:

  • Protein — 4 calories per gram. Builds and repairs muscle, keeps you full, and requires more energy to digest than the other macros (the thermic effect of food). Target: 1.6–2.2 g/kg of body weight for active adults, 0.8 g/kg for sedentary.
  • Carbohydrates — 4 calories per gram. Primary fuel for high-intensity exercise and brain function. Sources matter: whole grains, legumes, fruit and vegetables are more nutrient-dense than refined sugars.
  • Fat — 9 calories per gram. Hormone production, vitamin absorption (A, D, E, K), cell membrane structure, and satiety. Aim for mostly unsaturated fats — olive oil, nuts, avocado, fatty fish.

When the numbers are wrong

The Mifflin-St Jeor equation is the best available estimate for most people, but it will be off for some. Common reasons:

  • Very muscular people. The equation underestimates because muscle burns more at rest. Add 5–10%.
  • Very obese people. The equation tends to overestimate because a higher fraction of body weight is fat, which burns less at rest than the equation assumes.
  • People with thyroid conditions. Hypothyroidism lowers BMR by 5–15%. Hyperthyroidism raises it.
  • People on certain medications. Beta-blockers, antidepressants, corticosteroids and antipsychotics can change metabolic rate.
  • People over 60. The equation tends to overestimate slightly in older adults because of age-related muscle loss.
  • People who overestimate activity. The single most common source of error. "Moderately active" is not 5 workouts a week plus a desk job plus weekend hiking.

The fix: use the number as a starting point, track actual weight change for 2 weeks, and adjust by ±100–200 calories depending on which direction you're drifting.

How to actually use this number

  1. Track your food for a week. Don't change anything — just log what you actually eat. This shows your real baseline.
  2. Compare to your TDEE. If your logged average matches your TDEE, you're in maintenance. If it's higher, you're gaining. If lower, losing.
  3. Set a target. Aim for a deficit of 500 cal/day to lose weight, a surplus of 250–500 to gain muscle, or your TDEE to maintain.
  4. Track for 2 weeks. Weigh yourself at the same time each day and average the week. Actual change vs expected change tells you whether your target is right.
  5. Adjust slowly. If you're not losing at the expected rate, subtract 100–200 calories or add 1,000 steps. Do not crash diet.
  6. Recalculate every 4–8 weeks. As your weight changes, so does your TDEE.
  7. Prioritise protein and fibre. Both keep you full, which makes any calorie target easier to stick to.

Your privacy is protected

Every calculation runs in your browser. No age, weight, height or health information is sent to any server. Your inputs are stored only in your browser's local storage on your own device so they persist between visits — they never leave your computer or phone.

This calculator does not use cookies, does not track you across sites, and does not have access to any other health data on your device.

What this calculator can't do

Being honest about limits is more useful than pretending they don't exist. This calculator does not:

  • Diagnose any health condition. It gives estimates, not medical advice.
  • Measure body fat or muscle mass. For body composition, use DEXA, hydrostatic weighing, or a calibrated bioimpedance scale.
  • Replace a dietitian. For medical nutrition therapy, food allergies, eating disorders, or complex medical conditions, work with a registered dietitian.
  • Work for pregnancy or breastfeeding. Calorie needs are higher and vary by trimester and feeding status.
  • Work for children and teenagers. Growing bodies have different requirements and use age- and sex-specific reference values.
  • Account for medical conditions. Thyroid disorders, PCOS, diabetes, and medications all affect metabolism.
  • Track your food. This calculator gives the target. You still need a food log to hit it consistently.
  • Predict individual outcomes. The math is deterministic; human bodies are not. Expect real-world results to vary.

Common questions

What is BMR?

Basal Metabolic Rate is the number of calories your body burns at complete rest to maintain basic functions like breathing, circulation and cell repair. Even if you stayed in bed all day, you would burn this many calories.

What is TDEE?

Total Daily Energy Expenditure is BMR multiplied by an activity factor. It is the total calories you burn in a typical day including exercise and daily movement. It is the number you eat to maintain your current weight.

How many calories to lose weight?

A deficit of 500 calories per day produces roughly 0.5 kg of fat loss per week. Most guidelines suggest 0.5 to 1 kg per week maximum for sustainable weight loss. Faster loss tends to rebound and strip muscle.

How many calories to gain muscle?

A surplus of 250 to 500 calories per day supports muscle gain with minimal fat accumulation. Combined with resistance training and adequate protein (1.6 to 2.2 g per kg of body weight).

What equation does this calculator use?

The calculator uses the Mifflin-St Jeor equation, published in 1990. It is widely considered the most accurate BMR formula for the general population.

Why are the numbers different from another calculator?

Different calculators use different BMR formulas. Older formulas like Harris-Benedict or Katch-McArdle give different results. Activity multipliers can also vary. The Mifflin-St Jeor formula used here is the most widely recommended for healthy adults.

How accurate is this calculator?

It is a good estimate for most people. Actual calorie needs vary with genetics, body composition, medication, hormonal state and how intensely you actually train. Use the number as a starting point and adjust after two weeks of tracking.

Should I eat back exercise calories?

If your TDEE already includes an activity multiplier for exercise, do not add back exercise calories — you would be double counting. Most calorie trackers conflate these. This calculator already accounts for activity in the TDEE.

What is a safe calorie deficit?

A deficit of 500 calories per day (about 0.5 kg per week) is safe for most adults. Never go below your BMR for extended periods without medical supervision. Very low calorie diets below 1200 calories for women or 1500 for men should only be done under professional supervision.

Does the calculator work for athletes?

Very muscular athletes may need more calories than the equation predicts, because muscle burns more at rest. If you are very lean and muscular, add 5 to 10% to the TDEE. For very accurate athlete numbers, use a formula that includes body fat percentage like Katch-McArdle.

How much protein do I need?

For active adults, 1.6 to 2.2 grams of protein per kilogram of body weight per day supports muscle maintenance and growth. Sedentary adults need about 0.8 g/kg. Athletes in a calorie deficit may benefit from the higher end of the range.

Is keto safe?

A ketogenic diet (very low carb, high fat) can be effective for some people, but it is not right for everyone. It requires medical supervision for people with diabetes, kidney disease or lipid disorders. Speak to a doctor or dietitian before starting.

Should I count calories forever?

No. Most people use a calculator like this for 4 to 12 weeks to learn portion sizes and calorie awareness. After that, you can eat intuitively. Tracking every meal forever is not sustainable.

Does this calculator work for pregnant women?

No. Calorie needs during pregnancy and breastfeeding are higher and vary by trimester and feeding status. Use a pregnancy-specific calculator or your doctor's guidance.

Is my data saved?

Your inputs are stored only in your browser's local storage on your own device so they persist between visits. Nothing is sent to any server.

Does it work offline?

Yes. After the page has loaded once, the calculator works without an internet connection.

Is this calculator free?

Yes. It is completely free, runs entirely in your browser, and requires no signup or account.
